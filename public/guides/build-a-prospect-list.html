How to build a prospect list when you don't have an SDR

Most guides to prospect lists assume you have a sales-ops team, a data subscription, and someone whose full-time job is building lists. This one is written for the more common situation: you're the sales leader, or one of two to ten reps, and list-building happens in the gaps between everything else.

Updated September 2026.

In this guide
  1. Define your ICP in one sentence
  2. Pick 2–3 filters, not ten

The core idea: a list of 25 well-matched, well-researched contacts that actually gets worked will outperform a list of 500 that gets abandoned after the first page. Build for follow-through, not volume.

Step 1
Define your ICP in one sentence

Before opening any tool, write one sentence describing exactly who you sell to. Not "small businesses" or "sales leaders" — specific enough that you could hand the sentence to someone else and they'd build the same list you would.

Too vague: "Sales leaders at small companies."
Specific enough: "VP of Sales or Head of Sales at B2B SaaS companies, 20–200 employees, in the US, who don't already have a dedicated SDR team."

The qualifying detail at the end matters most. It's the difference between a list that's technically correct and a list where every contact actually needs what you sell.

Step 2
Pick 2–3 firmographic filters, not ten

It's tempting to filter on everything you can think of: industry, size, location, funding stage, tech stack, growth rate. Each additional filter narrows the list — and in a small dataset, over-filtering quietly removes good-fit prospects along with bad ones.

Pick the two or three filters that actually predict fit. For most small teams, that's industry, company size, and geography. Everything else is better handled in Step 6, when you're researching a specific person, not filtering a whole list.

Step 3
Search in plain language before you touch a filter tree

If your tool supports it, state your ICP as a sentence and search on that directly, rather than starting from an empty filter builder and reconstructing the same sentence one dropdown at a time. It's faster, and it keeps you anchored to the actual person you're looking for instead of the fields a database happens to have.

Step 4
Reveal in small batches and check quality before spending more

Whatever tool you're using, resist revealing your entire monthly allowance in one search. Pull 10 to 20 contacts first, and actually check them: are the titles current, is the company right, does the seniority match what you asked for. If the batch looks off, adjust your search before you spend the rest of your budget on the same mismatch.

Step 5
Score and prioritize before you write anything

Not every contact on a qualified list deserves the same effort. Before drafting a single message, rank what you have:

  • Seniority — someone who can actually decide or strongly influence the purchase, not just someone with an adjacent title
  • Verified contact info — a confirmed email or phone beats a guessed one; don't spend your best message on a contact you might not reach
  • Fit to your ICP sentence — how closely this specific person and company match what you wrote in Step 1, not just whether they cleared the filters

Work the top of that ranking first. A short list worked in the right order beats a long list worked in the order it happened to load.

Step 6
Research each contact before the first message

This is the step most small teams skip, and it's the one that determines whether the list produces replies. Before reaching out, spend two minutes on each contact: what does their role actually involve day to day, what are they likely measured on, and what's one thing about their company right now that gives you a real reason to write to them.

A message that references something specific about the person gets answered. A message that could have been sent to anyone with the same job title gets ignored, no matter how good the list underneath it was.

Step 7
Keep the list somewhere it won't die in a spreadsheet

The most common failure mode isn't a bad list — it's a good list that gets built once, worked halfway through, and then buried in a spreadsheet tab nobody reopens. Whatever you use to hold the list, make sure it tracks status per contact (not contacted, contacted, replied, no response) so prospecting has a state you can pick back up instead of starting over from a blank search every time momentum stalls.

Common questions

How many prospects should be on a first list?

Start smaller than feels ambitious: 20 to 30 well-matched contacts you can research and reach out to properly beats 500 you'll never get through. A tight, well-qualified list that gets worked completely outperforms a large list that gets ignored after the first ten.

What's the difference between a lead list and a prospect list?

A lead list is typically names and company data with no qualification behind it, often bought in bulk. A prospect list is qualified against your ICP and prioritized by fit, so every entry is someone you have an actual reason to contact.

How often should a small team refresh its prospect list?

Weekly in small batches works better than a large quarterly pull. Contacts change roles, companies change size, and a list built once a quarter is stale by the time half of it gets worked.
