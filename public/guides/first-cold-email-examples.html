What to say in a first cold email

Most cold email advice is about formatting: subject lines, send times, follow-up cadence. That's real, but it's not why most cold emails fail. They fail because of what's actually written in them. Here's the structure that works, three real examples, and the mistake that kills more first emails than a bad subject line ever could.

The core idea: a cold email isn't judged on how well-written it is. It's judged in about two seconds on one question — does this person clearly know who I am and why they're writing to me specifically. Everything else is secondary.

The mistake that kills most cold emails

Open with your company. "Hi [Name], I'm reaching out from [Company] — we help businesses like yours [do a thing]." It's the most common opening in cold email, and it's the fastest way to get deleted, because it tells the recipient nothing about themselves — only about you.

The recipient's actual question, whether they'd say it out loud or not, is: why is this relevant to me, specifically, right now? An opening about your company can't answer that. An opening about their situation can.

A structure that works

Four parts, in this order:

  • A specific observation about them. Something true about their role, their company, or something they've done recently — specific enough that it couldn't apply to a random competitor's VP of Sales.
  • Why that observation connects to a real problem. One sentence bridging the observation to something they likely deal with.
  • What you do, in one sentence. Not a paragraph. One line, plainly stated.
  • A small, specific ask. Not "let's hop on a call" — something lower-friction. See the call-to-action section below.

That's it. No paragraph of credentials, no bullet list of features, no case study in the first message. Those all belong later, once someone has actually replied.

Three examples

Example — sales leader, small team
Subject: Prospecting at Northline
Hi Dana — saw Northline's been hiring reps steadily this year, which usually means prospecting starts falling on whoever has time rather than a consistent process. Quick question: when your team reaches out cold today, do they know much about the person before the first message, or is it mostly name and title? I run a tool built for teams your size that fixes exactly that gap. Worth a two-line reply either way — curious how you're handling it now.
Why this works: the observation (hiring reps) is specific and true, the question is easy to answer in one line, and the ask ("two-line reply") is small enough that ignoring it feels ruder than answering it.
Example — owner-operator, does own sales
Subject: Quick one, Marcus
Marcus — noticed Reyes Mechanical picked up the new distribution contract last month. Growth like that usually means more time in the field and less time finding the next ten accounts. I built a tool that finds the right contacts and tells you what to say to them before you call, so prospecting takes minutes instead of an evening you don't have. If that's a real problem right now, happy to show you in five minutes. If not, no worries at all.
Why this works: speaks directly to a solo operator's actual constraint — time — rather than generic sales pain, and the closing line removes pressure instead of adding it.
What to avoid
Subject: Boost Your Sales Pipeline Today!
Hi there, My name is Alex and I'm with GrowthTech Solutions. We help companies like yours increase revenue through cutting-edge AI-powered prospecting technology. Our platform has helped hundreds of businesses 10x their pipeline. I'd love to schedule a 30-minute call this week to show you a demo. Are you free Tuesday or Wednesday? Best, Alex
Why this fails: "Hi there" signals a mail-merge before the first sentence is even read. Nothing in it is specific to the recipient — it's entirely about the sender. And a 30-minute call is a large ask from someone who has given no reason to trust the sender yet.

Subject lines

The job of a subject line is to get opened, nothing more. Short and specific beats clever almost every time. A first name, a company name, or a plain description of the topic — "Prospecting at Northline," "Quick one, Marcus" — outperforms anything that sounds like marketing copy. If a subject line could be in a spam folder's greatest hits ("Boost your sales pipeline today!"), rewrite it.

The call to action

Most first cold emails ask for too much. "Let's schedule 30 minutes" is a real commitment from someone who doesn't know you yet, and it's an easy thing to leave unanswered because saying no explicitly feels awkward — so the recipient just doesn't reply.

A smaller ask gets more replies and still moves things forward:

  • "Worth a two-line reply either way?"
  • "Is this even the right problem for your team, or am I off base?"
  • "If this is relevant, I'll send more. If not, no worries."

Once someone replies to any of these, even a one-word answer, you have a real conversation instead of a cold email — and asking for time becomes far easier.

Common questions

How long should a cold email be?

Under 100 words for a first message. Long enough to be specific, short enough to read on a phone in ten seconds. If it takes longer than that to read, it usually takes longer than that to get deleted.

Should a cold email include a call to action?

Yes, but keep it small. Asking for a 15-minute call is a bigger commitment than most first-time recipients will make. A lower-friction ask, like a one-word reply or a yes/no question, gets more responses and still moves the conversation forward.

What's the biggest mistake in cold emails?

Leading with your company and product instead of the recipient's situation. A first email that could have been sent to anyone with the same job title reads as a mail-merge, even when it wasn't one, and gets ignored regardless of how good the offer is.

Do personalized cold emails actually get more replies?

Personalization that's specific and relevant to the recipient's actual role or situation consistently outperforms generic templates, because it signals the message was written for them rather than blasted to a list. Personalization that's just their first name and company inserted into a template doesn't have the same effect, since recipients can tell the difference.
