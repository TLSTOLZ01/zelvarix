Most advice on this topic treats the decision maker as a single person hiding behind a gate, waiting to be found. From the outside, you cannot see who signs off, who influences the purchase and who would block it. So a better question than "who is the decision maker?" is "who should I contact first?" That one you can answer yourself, and answer well. This guide shows how, and where a contact discovery tool fits.

Start with who to target, not who "the" decision maker is

When you search for a decision maker, you are really making three choices:

  1. Department: which team owns the problem you solve?
  2. Seniority: how senior should the first person be, given the size of the company?
  3. Number of contacts: how many people will you write to at each account?

Make those three choices before you look up a single email address. Contact details are the easy part. Choosing the right person to write to is the part that decides whether anyone replies.

How to decide who to target

1. Start from the problem you solve, then pick the department

Write down the problem your product fixes and ask which team feels it every week. If you sell a tool for sales teams, that is sales. If you sell a reporting tool, it may be finance or operations. If you sell a way to reach customers, it may be marketing. Choose one department for your first list. You can add a second one later, once you know what the replies look like.

2. Pick seniority by company size

The same job title means different things in different companies. In a very small company, the owner may handle buying directly. In a larger one, the most senior person in a department may be several steps away from the daily problem and may never read your email. Use this table as a starting guess:

Company size (rough)Reasonable first seniority to tryContacts to start with
About 1 to 10 employeesOwner or founder1
About 11 to 50 employeesHead of the department that owns the problem1 or 2
About 51 to 200 employeesDirector or VP in that department1 or 2
More than 200 employeesDirector or senior manager in the specific department1 or 2, and expect more people to be involved

These are our editorial rules of thumb, not benchmarks, and they are not facts about any particular company. Test them against your own replies. If a reply says "wrong person, talk to this one", you have just learned something no database can tell you, so write it down.

3. Choose one or two contacts per account

Pick the person whose week is most affected by the problem you solve. If you add a second, choose a peer or manager who would also gain if that problem went away. Messaging a whole leadership team at once adds names to your list, not quality to your outreach.

Time is the other reason to stay small. The 7th edition of Salesforce's State of Sales report is based on a survey of 4,050 sales professionals across 22 countries, run from August through September 2025. The report states: "Sales reps spend almost one full day of their workweek on prospecting, and nearly half say it's one of the worst parts of working in sales." (page 11). The figures are what third-party panelists reported in a survey; nobody timed them. The point for list building: each extra contact per account eats into hours that are already tight. [Source 1]

How to find decision maker contact info in five steps

  1. Write your target in one sentence. For example: "[Seniority] in [department] at [industry] companies with [size range] employees in [state]." If you cannot finish the sentence, finish it before you search.
  2. Find the people in that role. Start with free places you can check yourself: a company's website and team pages, a person's public profile, and the company's own announcements. A contact discovery tool is built to shorten that lookup and leave more time for the steps below.
  3. Pick one or two per account. Use the table above, and keep a note of why you picked each person.
  4. Give each person a reason to hear from you. Look for one concrete detail about their company or role that you can check and that connects to the problem you solve. If nothing turns up, move the account to next week instead of sending a generic note.
  5. Get contact details only for the people you will actually write to. Do not collect every email you can find. Look up the details when you are ready to write, and record who you contacted and what happened. That record is how you tighten your target next week.

Whatever route you use, the method is the same. If you want the longer version of the list-building process, read how to build a prospect list when you don't have an SDR, and see how small sales teams build a short prospect list for how to size the list to your week.

Which job titles should a small sales team target?

It depends on what you sell and on company size, so treat these as starting points to test, not benchmarks. In very small companies, try the owner or a founder. In companies of roughly 11 to 50 people, try the head of the department that owns the problem. In companies of roughly 51 to 200 people, try a director or VP in that department. How many people should I contact at each company?

Begin with one, two at most: the person closest to the problem you solve and, optionally, a colleague who would also benefit if it were fixed. Widen the circle later, once a reply or a conversation points you to someone else.

Do AI sales tools fix a poorly chosen target list?

Not on their own. An AI tool that drafts emails or scores contacts works from whatever list you give it, so a poorly chosen list gets polished messages aimed at the wrong people.
