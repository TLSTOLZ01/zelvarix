How a Small Sales Team Can Prospect Without an SDR

Most prospecting advice assumes a dedicated SDR, a sales-ops person and a data subscription. Most small teams have none of those. They have a sales leader, a few reps, and a week already full of calls. Here is a routine that works anyway.

Published November 2026.

The short version: an SDR gives you protected time. You can create that yourself with a fixed weekly block, a short list and a simple record of who has been contacted.

Why prospecting stalls on small teams

It rarely stalls because nobody knows how to do it. It stalls because it is never urgent. Open deals always feel more important than the people who might become deals, so prospecting gets pushed to "when things slow down", and by then the pipeline is already thin.

A dedicated SDR fixes this by making prospecting someone's whole job. If you cannot hire one, you need to make it a routine instead of a task.

1. Put it on the calendar, every week

Pick a block of 60 to 90 minutes, at the same time each week, and treat it like a customer meeting. Mornings tend to work better than the end of a long day. If the block gets moved more than once a month, it is not a priority yet, and that is worth knowing.

2. Decide who you are looking for before you search

Write one sentence: the role, the type of company, the size and one detail that makes them a good fit. "Owners of managed IT firms with 10 to 50 employees" is a target. "Small businesses" is not.

Keep your filters few. Industry, company size and location are usually enough. Every extra filter shrinks the list and makes it less likely you will find anyone.

3. Work a short list, not a long one

Twenty to thirty well-matched contacts a week is plenty. The goal is not volume. It is finishing what you started. A big list that is half worked creates a feeling of being behind, and that feeling is what makes people stop.

4. Spend two minutes on each person before you write

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that decides whether anyone replies. Look at what the person's role involves, what they are likely measured on and whether anything recently changed at their company. Then open with that, not with who you are.

A first message that shows you did two minutes of homework stands out because so few do. Our guide with cold email examples shows what this looks like in practice.

5. Keep a record of who you contacted

You do not need a CRM for this. A simple list with one status per person is enough: not contacted, contacted, replied, no response. What matters is that you can pick it back up next week in ten seconds instead of starting from a blank search.

6. Follow up before you add new names

Most replies come from the second or third message, not the first. Before you add new contacts, go back to the ones who did not answer. This is the habit that separates a prospecting routine from a pile of one-time attempts.

7. Use tools that fit a team your size

The tool matters less than the routine, but the wrong one can quietly kill it. If it needs a training course, charges for every seat or only gives you names with no context, your reps will stop opening it. Look for something that is quick to start, priced for the whole team and tells you something about each person. Our roundup of prospecting tools without per-seat pricing compares the options.

What a good week looks like

  • Monday: 60 to 90 minutes of protected time on the calendar.
  • Build or refresh a short list of 20 to 30 matched contacts.
  • Spend two minutes researching each, then send a message that reflects it.
  • Mark each contact's status as you go.
  • Later in the week: follow up on last week's non-responders.

That is the whole system. It is not exciting, and that is the point. A routine you keep is worth more than a perfect plan you do not.

Common questions

Do I need an SDR to start prospecting?

No. An SDR mostly buys you protected time. A small team can get most of the benefit by scheduling a fixed block each week, working a short list, and keeping a simple record of who has been contacted.

How many prospects should a small team work each week?

Fewer than feels ambitious. Twenty to thirty well-matched contacts a week that get researched and followed up beat hundreds that never get touched. Raise the number only when the follow-up is keeping pace.

What is the biggest mistake small teams make with prospecting?

Treating it as something to do when the pipeline gets thin. Prospecting works as a routine, so a small block every week beats a large push every quarter.
